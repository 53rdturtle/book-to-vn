The Lantern at the Edge of the Wood

The sky over Hollowbrook was the colour of wet slate, and the wind carried the smell of pine resin down from the ridge. Mara stood at the edge of the wood with an unlit lantern in her hand and a letter folded three times in her pocket. She had read the letter twice on the walk from the village, and the creases were already beginning to soften.

"You're late," said a voice from the trees.

Mara did not startle. She had been expecting the voice, even if she had not been expecting it to come from the left. She turned toward it and saw the old man step out from behind a grey birch, his coat buttoned wrong and his beard full of burrs.

"I was not late," Mara said. "You were early. There is a difference, and we should be precise about it tonight of all nights."

"Precision," the old man said. He made the word sound like something he had once believed in and then misplaced. "Very well. I was early. Did you bring it?"

Mara took the letter from her pocket but did not offer it. The wind pulled at the corner of the paper as if it, too, wanted to read what was written there.

"Before I give it to you," she said, "I want you to tell me what my mother wrote on the back."

The old man was quiet for a long time. Somewhere deeper in the wood, an owl called once and then thought better of it. He looked at Mara's lantern, and then at her face, and then at the ground between them.

"She wrote, 'If he asks, tell him I forgave him in the end, but not for his sake.'"

Mara's hand did not shake, but it wanted to. She held the letter out. The old man took it with both hands, the way a man takes a thing he has been afraid of for years.

"Light the lantern," he said quietly. "Please. I don't want to read it in the dark."

She struck a match against the iron of the lantern's base. The flame caught, steadied, and threw a small warm circle onto the forest floor. In that circle the old man unfolded the letter, and for a moment Mara thought she saw, on his face, the particular stillness of a man who has finally been given permission to grieve.

"Thank you," he said, without looking up. "Thank you, Mara. You didn't have to come."

"I know," she said. "That's why I came."

The owl tried again, and this time it finished the call. Above them, the slate sky began, very slowly, to let go of the first few stars.
